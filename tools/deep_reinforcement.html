Deep Reinforcement Learning

Understanding how AI agents learn to interact with dynamic environments through trial, error, and probability.

Moving Beyond Supervised Learning

Unlike traditional deep learning paradigms where models learn to map static inputs to outputs, Deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) involves an active model interacting dynamically with its environment. The ultimate goal is to learn a policy that maximizes future rewards over many continuous time steps.

Value Learning & DQN

The Q-Function captures the expected total future reward an agent can receive by executing a certain action given a state. A Deep Q-Network (DQN) uses a neural network to estimate these values for highly complex spaces.

Q(st, at) = E[ Rt | st, at ]
⚠️
Compute Warning: The Bicycle Example

If we attempted to build a traditional Q-table and mathematically calculate the true expected future reward for every possible raw pixel combination and every continuous distance measurement in our bicycle environment, the massive amount of calculation required would instantly freeze our systems—the computer would simply "die to compute." This explosion of states is exactly why this interactive simulator is a sampled approximation, and why Deep Neural Networks are strictly required to estimate Q-functions in real-world scenarios.

Downsides: Q-Learning cannot handle continuous action spaces, as it requires a distinct, predefined output list (e.g., exclusively Left, Right, or Straight). It is also entirely deterministic, failing to learn stochastic, adaptable policies.

Policy Learning & Continuous Actions

Instead of predicting static mathematical values for distinct choices, Policy Gradients output a probability distribution. This enables the modeling of infinite continuous actions (like precise steering wheel angles) by parameterizing a Gaussian curve defined by a Mean and Variance. The agent then samples from this curve to act.

π(s) ~ P(a|s) = N(μ, σ²)

Policy Gradient Update (Gradient Ascent) w' = w + ∇ log P(at | st) * Rt
